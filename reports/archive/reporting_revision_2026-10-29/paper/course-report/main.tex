Context, Weights, and Activation Survival in Gemma-2-2B}
\author{Hemang Joshi \and Eashaan Thakur \and Shardul Joshi \and Nikhita Ravi\\
International Institute of Information Technology, Hyderabad}

\begin{abstract}
Quantization changes sparse autoencoder (SAE) activations, but an activation change need not change what an intervention on that feature does. In Gemma-2-2B with a frozen layer-12 Gemma Scope SAE, 27 FLORES-200 languages and round-to-nearest weight quantization, we first show that activation survival, language-association retention and causal contribution are different outcomes: 70.8\% of established language features keep their peak language at INT4, removing 64-feature language groups raises own-text loss beyond matched controls in all 27 languages, and individual feature effects drift as much for matched other directions. We then cross the two things quantization changes, the activations before an intervention (context) and the weights after it, for a fixed feature ablation. At INT6 the effect changes little (cosine 0.98, an error of about two numerical floors, above the rounding floor). At INT4 the group-level harm is amplified (median net harm 0.85 against 0.26 for the fixed full-precision edit); the context term of that edit is about three times the weight term ($|C|=0.31$, $|W|=0.10$), but with the quantized state's own activation the harm (0.43) is close to that of the weights alone in the median language, and the effect vector of single features is rotated (cosine 0.77) with the larger norm in the weight term, a substantial interaction, and largely the same rotation for other directions. Averaged fingerprints overstate transfer (+0.126 cosine). The sensitivity to other directions predicts the held-out error (21.5\% reduction at INT4, an interval including zero at INT6, and a similar gain in a rounding-only state); on eligible items at INT4 neither activation survival nor gradient attribution adds measurably. A six-language Wikipedia panel repeats the INT4 statements; at INT6 its group-level weight term is the larger one and the prediction criterion is met. Apart from one family, all analyses are exploratory; one model, one SAE, simulated quantization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Weight quantization reduces the numerical precision of language-model parameters, and its effects need not be uniform across languages or tasks \citep{marchisio-etal-2024-quantization}. Internal measurements can complement task metrics, but only if their interpretation is explicit. Calling a sparse autoencoder (SAE) feature ``lost'' can mean a changed activation pattern, a changed preferred language, or a changed effect on predictions. SAEs decompose an activation into sparse directions \citep{cunningham2023}; language-associated features have been identified and manipulated in full precision \citep{deng-etal-2025-unveiling}; and \citet{duan2026} measure activation correlations before and after weight quantization with a frozen SAE, leaving open whether interventions built on those features survive. The representation can change while a contribution remains, or an intervention can change because the downstream model changes.

We began by asking whether language-selective features were more vulnerable than non-selective controls and whether that explained resource-tier disparities. After implementation audits and negative primary contrasts we expanded the project to test the measurement assumptions directly, and then to its main question. This report presents the whole investigation, including hypotheses that were not supported. \textbf{RQ1:} how do activation preservation and language-association retention compare? \textbf{RQ2:} do selected language groups contribute to prediction and comprehension, and how does that change with precision? \textbf{RQ3:} do individual intervention effects support an interpretation of feature-specific damage? \textbf{RQ4:} does the group dependence appear on a second model? \textbf{RQ5 (main):} does the effect of a \emph{fixed} intervention survive quantization, and is a change due to the context, the weights, or their interaction; can it be predicted for held-out features?

Our contribution is a controlled case study: native interventions, donor patches and effect fingerprints across weight precisions (Sections 4, 6 and 8), and a crossed context--weights design for a fixed feature intervention with matched and random directions, a numerical floor, a held-out prediction target and an external-text check (Section 7). Ablation, patching and fingerprints are not new methods; the outcome is a set of distinctions and counterexamples to reading activation survival as functional survival, within one model and one measurement basis.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Multilingual quantization.}
\citet{marchisio-etal-2024-quantization} show that task, language and evaluation method affect the observed degradation; their behavioural evidence motivates our language panel but does not identify SAE features as its mechanism. Resource categories are a descriptive grouping \citep{joshi-etal-2020-state}, not controlled interventions on training data.

\paragraph{Language-associated features.}
\citet{deng-etal-2025-unveiling} identify language-specific SAE features and use ablation and steering, so an own-language loss effect in full precision is supporting evidence, not a novelty claim. Gemma Scope supplies open SAEs across Gemma 2 layers \citep{lieberum-etal-2024-gemma}. A frozen dictionary gives consistent coordinates across precision states without guaranteeing that each coordinate is an invariant mechanism.

\paragraph{Compression and causal interpretation.}
\citet{duan2026} measure feature survival under round-to-nearest quantization by activation correlation. A pruning study already separates geometric survival from causal importance \citep{borobia2026}. \citet{chang-etal-2025-inputs} use activation patching to localize quantization error and compare the loss recovered by patching activations with that recovered by restoring weights (their Section 6), which is why a context-dominated result alone is not our contribution; \citet{hoang2026} analyze the geometry of intervention-induced logit changes; and \citet{vaidyanathan2026} show that the effect estimated by activation patching contains an interaction term that is a diagnostic rather than noise, small when the model is locally affine. We add a crossed design for a fixed SAE intervention under quantization, controls for generic drift, and a held-out prediction target. Belebele \citep{bandarkar-etal-2024-belebele} supplies a separate comprehension outcome.

\section{Setting and Research History}
We use Gemma-2-2B \citep{gemma2024} and a 16,384-feature JumpReLU \citep{rajamanoharan2024} Gemma Scope SAE at the layer-12 post-block residual stream; the model runs in bfloat16, the SAE and all edits in float32, and the same dictionary serves every condition. Quantization uses the vendored reference round-to-nearest (RTN) functions (output-channel scaling), restoring the original weights before each state; values stay in floating-point storage, so we do not evaluate integer kernels. States are full precision (FP), INT8 to INT4, and INT16 and INT12 as near-lossless rounding references (re-rounding bfloat16 weights still changes them). One BOS token is prepended; BOS and padding are excluded from statistics and edits.

The panel is 27 parallel FLORES-200 \citep{nllb2022} languages in three descriptive tiers of nine (Appendix~\ref{app:languages}). Features are discovered on the development split; association studies use 27,036 content tokens per language on devtest; the loss evaluation uses 300 aligned devtest sentences per language. Devtest had been examined earlier, so all FLORES functional analyses are exploratory. Belebele items are split by passage origin into calibration and evaluation; calibration selects the answer format and 18 languages with accuracy above chance plus 0.05, and the frozen evaluation has 385 items per language.

An initial implementation was retired after an audit; its outputs are not used. A rule is \emph{pre-specified} if committed to the repository before the data it judges were scored; none was registered externally. The only confirmatory family is the Belebele family (its first statement needs a positive FP macro effect; 99\% intervals for the five retention statements); everything else is exploratory.

\section{Measurements and Interventions}
\paragraph{Activation and association.}
With $a_f^b(t)$ the activation of feature $f$ at token $t$ in state $b$, the preservation score is $c_f^b=\operatorname{corr}_t(a_f^{\mathrm{FP}}(t),a_f^b(t))$ and the historical survival cutoff is $c_f^b>0.9$. Language selectivity uses firing rates $r_{\ell f}$ and $S_f=\max_\ell r_{\ell f}/\operatorname{mean}_\ell r_{\ell f}$, with a shuffled-language null (95th percentile, 50 permutations), at least 50 peak tokens, a panel rate of at least $10^{-5}$ and the same peak in 80 of 100 resamples; the cohort has 8,086 features. A feature is \emph{established} on devtest if its original language is the unique supported firing-rate peak, and \emph{retained} at $b$ if that stays so; retention conditions on FP establishment. We also analyze the FP margin to the strongest competing language.

\paragraph{Groups, controls and edits.}
$G_\ell$ is the 64 highest-activation selective features of language $\ell$ on dev. Controls are matched greedily on log firing rate and log mean activation from 2,860 supported non-selective features (five draws; three in Study G). Matching is imperfect (75 of 81 language--size ratios within $[0.8,1.25]$; Thai, Yoruba and Lao outside at K64), so activity matching is not an identification argument for language specificity. With $x$ a residual, $a$ its SAE encoding and $d_f$ a decoder row, edits preserve the reconstruction error:
\begin{equation}
 x'=x+\sum_{f\in G}(a'_f-a_f)d_f,
 \label{eq:edit}
\end{equation}
zero ablation sets $a'_f=0$ and scaling $a'_f=\alpha a_f$; identity and sham edits check the path. For donor patches the recipient is quantized and $a'_f$ is the FP activation at the same position.

\paragraph{Effects and uncertainty.}
Harm is the change of the negative log-likelihood against the same state's unedited prediction (nats per token; Belebele nats per item). The group effect is
\begin{equation}
 E_\ell^b=h_\ell^b(G_\ell)-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}h_\ell^b(C_{\ell r}),
 \label{eq:effect}
\end{equation}
negative harm is improvement and never clipped. Retention is $E_\ell^b/E_\ell^{\mathrm{FP}}$ for languages with a clearly positive FP effect, averaged per language (a mean of ratios; it differs from the ratio of means). Loss intervals are 2,000 paired cluster-bootstrap draws over aligned sentence identifiers; Belebele resamples passages; association intervals use 1,000 sentence resamples and can be shifted by near-tied peaks.

\section{Replication and Initial Tests}
The vendored WikiText replication matches the published table closely (INT7 81.19\% against 81.26\%; INT6 51.15\% against 51.30\%), but the no-BOS FP perplexity is 458.4; with BOS added and masked it is about 16.1 and INT6 survival on WikiText is near 97.5\%. This shows sensitivity to the input regime, not that every published result is invalid, so we use BOS-masked evaluation and keep the no-BOS study as history. In that primary regime none of the three planned tests rejects after Holm correction (selective-minus-control survival $-0.28$ and $-1.44$ points at INT7 and INT6; adjusted $p=0.514$, $0.128$); a stricter post hoc threshold gives $-7.16$ at INT6, and matching activation magnitude reverses it to $+4.58$. The tier disparity (INT6 survival 69.4/90.6/95.1\% for low/mid/high) attenuates from $+12.3$ to $-0.81$ points per step after conditioning on activation scale ($p=0.667$), which is correlational and does not identify the cause of multilingual damage.

\section{Association and Functional Results}
\paragraph{Peaks persist beyond correlation survival.}
Only 6,663 of 8,086 selected features (82.4\%) establish their peak on FP devtest; among them, retention falls gradually to 70.8\% at INT4 (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}); the INT16 reference loses 56 features (99.2\% retained) and no established feature becomes inactive. Correlation and association disagree: at INT6, 75.8\% of association-lost features still exceed the correlation cutoff; at INT4, 79\% of retained ones do not. The FP margin predicts association loss within a language (AUROC 0.90, 0.81, 0.75 at INT6/5/4; most borderline fifth against least at INT6: 20.7\% against 0.1\%), so much of the loss is sensitivity to near ties.

\paragraph{Group dependence on loss, and little localized damage.}
Zeroing a language's K64 group raises own-text loss beyond controls in all 27 languages at every precision: FP macro effect 0.704 nats/token $[0.684,0.723]$ (median 0.262), retention near one through INT6, 1.13 at INT5 and 2.14 at INT4 $[2.04,2.50]$ (the ratio of macro effects is only 1.33). This is dependence of a damaged model on the group, not better language ability. Restoring the group with FP activations at INT4 recovers 2.6\% of the loss gap (controls 1.2\%; all SAE features 17.4\%; whole residual 39.3\%), and about nothing at INT5 and INT6: necessity within a model is not responsibility for the difference between models. Matching association-lost and retained features on native activity shows no systematic harm difference (median lost-minus-retained $+0.0006$, $+0.0007$, $+0.0002$ nats/token at INT4/5/6).

\paragraph{The comprehension statement is not met.}
On frozen Belebele the FP macro group effect is $-0.0001$ nats/item $[-0.0035,+0.0031]$; accuracy is 0.426 unedited, 0.426 with the group ablated and 0.425 with controls. No covered language has a clearly positive FP effect, so statement S0 is not met and S1--S5 are undefined; the whole group ($+0.0007$ $[-0.0030,+0.0042]$) agrees. Random sets of 1,024--2,860 features lower accuracy by 1.2--6.0 points while sham edits change nothing, so the edit path is sensitive and the null cannot exclude group effects of about half a point. Our frozen prompt scores above the lm-evaluation-harness prompt in all 18 languages (point estimates).

\paragraph{Individual effects drift, but directions drift too.}
For 1,487 features the median cosine between FP and quantized effect fingerprints is 0.997/0.988/0.961/0.839 at INT8/6/5/4 (fraction above the INT16 reference 94.0/89.7/66.5/0.3\%), yet each feature's effect stays far closer to its own FP fingerprint than to others' (retrieval 0.997--1.000). In the 404-feature control that subtracts another matched decoder direction at the same positions and amplitudes the medians are 0.842 and 0.828 at INT4 (differences of medians 0.004--0.007 at INT8/6/5), so most drift is shared by arbitrary perturbations. About 31\% of features above the write-side reference fail the activation-survival cutoff at INT5, and association status has descriptive AUROC 0.506 for INT4 effect similarity (at INT6 on the F5 sample the FP margin predicts association loss at 0.86 but function change at 0.58): label stability and causal-effect stability are different.

\section{Intervention Transfer under Quantization}
\label{sec:transfer}
\paragraph{Design.}
The comparisons above change activations and weights together. We instead ask how the effect of a \emph{fixed} intervention changes when the model is quantized, and whether the change comes from the context (the residual and SAE activations before the edit), the weights (the layers after it) or their interaction. At the positions where a feature is active in FP we subtract its FP decoder contribution (float32; every cell subtracts its own unedited baseline; a zero edit is exactly zero). Let $D[p,s]$ be the mean centered change of the output logits with prefix state $p$ and suffix state $s$, each FP or quantized (Q). Then $C=D[\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{FP}]-D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]$, $W=D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{Q}]-D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]$, $I=D[\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{Q}]-D[\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{FP}]-D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{Q}]+D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]$, and $D[\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{Q}]-D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]=C+W+I$ exactly. A fifth cell uses the quantized state's own activation (native amplitude).

\paragraph{Controls, floor, populations.}
Each edit is repeated along a matched swapped direction (another feature's decoder row) and three random norm-matched orthogonal directions. Because the model runs in bfloat16, even INT16 changes the logits; per direction kind the numerical floor is the 95th percentile of the INT16 error over calibration features (28.7 for the real direction), and an item is \emph{eligible} if its FP effect norm exceeds five floors. The normalized error is $\lVert D[\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{Q}]-D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]\rVert/(\lVert D[\mathrm{FP},\mathrm{FP}]\rVert+\text{floor})$. The feature level has 404 features with up to 16 contexts each (6,464 feature--context pairs; 2,203 eligible real-direction items of 235 features), split by a hash of the feature id into calibration and held-out halves; the group level ablates each language's K64 group and three matched control groups on the 300 evaluation sentences of the 27 languages and decomposes the net harm of Equation~\ref{eq:effect}. Intervals are 2,000-replicate crossed bootstraps over features and source-sentence clusters (group level: sentences only). Pre-declared: A3, the expectation that the context term dominates the weight term at the group level; H1, that the error is not explained by a rescaling of the FP effect or by the quantized state's own activation; H2 (descriptive), that $C$, $W$, $I$ reproduce between the calibration and the held-out half; H3, that the generic sensitivity (below) predicts the error beyond activation measurements; H4, that the averaged fingerprint transfers differently from its single contexts. The design, thresholds and hypotheses were frozen and hashed before the first quantized run; amendments made after a 12-feature instrument run and before the full runs concern a gate threshold, the form of the gradient baseline and one extra baseline column, and the claim rules were tightened once after an independent code review and before the outcomes were read (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}).

\paragraph{Group level.}
At INT6 the terms are small and equal (median $|C|=0.0091$ $[0.0056,0.0114]$, $|W|=0.0092$ $[0.0061,0.0133]$; the context term is larger in 12 of 27 languages), about 3.5\% of the FP net harm and about 19 and 35 times the rounding-only values, so the group effect changes little (net harm 0.281 against 0.263). At INT4 the median net harm is 0.85 with the fixed FP edit, and for that edit the context term is about three times the weight term ($|C|=0.310$ $[0.258,0.334]$, $|W|=0.104$ $[0.094,0.122]$, $|I|=0.071$); the interval of the median $|C|-|W|$ is $[+0.022,+0.073]$ (the context term is larger in 19 languages, as in the rounding-only state, so the count is not evidence; the interval is). The expectation that context dominates (A3) is supported at INT4 for this edit and undecided at INT6. A caveat matters for the reading: the median net harm of the four cells is 0.263 (FP/FP), 0.684 (quantized context, FP weights), 0.428 (FP context, quantized weights) and 0.848 (both), and with the quantized state's own activation 0.433. Paired per language (post hoc), native minus fixed is $-0.35$ $[-0.36,-0.29]$ (all 27 languages) and native minus weights-only $+0.014$ $[0.009,0.023]$ in the median, ranging from $-0.78$ to $+0.23$: close to the weights in the median language, not in every one; the context term of the fixed edit comes from subtracting a full-precision-sized contribution in a context where the feature's activation has changed.

\paragraph{Feature level.}
At INT6 the effect vector is rotated little (cosine 0.984, normalized error 0.165, 1.9 floors: small, but above the rounding floor); at INT4 it is rotated strongly (cosine 0.768) with a slightly larger norm (ratio 1.09) and a normalized error of 0.664 $[0.647,0.680]$, 7.7 floors. The error is almost orthogonal to the FP effect (97--99\% at every state, 99.7\% at INT16) and using the quantized state's own activation leaves 97\% of it, so neither a rescaling nor an activation change explains it (H1: the shares alone say nothing because they are equal in the rounding-only state, hence the requirement that the error clears the floor). Unlike the group-level loss, the \emph{weight} term has the larger norm in the effect vector (relative norms 0.145 against 0.100 at INT6, 0.560 against 0.359 at INT4; $\lVert C\rVert>\lVert W\rVert$ in 6\% and 10\% of items; exploratory, the terms are not additive), and the interaction is a third of the change at INT4. The two readings differ in what they measure: the norm of a mean logit change against a signed loss summed over a group. Most of the error is not specific to the feature's direction: matched and random directions give terms and errors of similar size, with small differences in both directions (INT4: the real direction's normalized error is above its controls by $+0.019$ $[0.009,0.032]$; the controls are not eligibility-filtered, so this mixes scale and specificity: in the 144 pairs with matched FP effect size the real direction's error norm is $\times1.12$ (INT6) and $\times1.14$ (INT4) that of its swapped direction ($\times1.00$ at INT16): a modest specific part, post hoc).

\paragraph{Averages hide the contexts.}
The cosine of a feature's \emph{averaged} fingerprint exceeds the mean cosine of its single contexts by 0.126 $[0.121,0.131]$ at INT4, 0.024 at INT6 and 0.0145 $[0.011,0.017]$ in the rounding-only state (Figure~\ref{fig:terms}c; a positive gap is expected from independent rounding noise); the averaged fingerprint also has the lower normalized error in all 404 features, in every state. Averaged fingerprints therefore overstate how well an intervention transfers (H4).

\paragraph{Prediction.}
We fit ridge models on the calibration half and score the held-out features (feature-held-out: their contexts share source sentences with the calibration half). A uses activation measurements (cosine of FP and quantized activations, log activation, firing share, log effect and residual norms), G adds the loss gradient at the edited rows, B adds the generic sensitivity measured with the \emph{other} four directions at the same context. B reduces the held-out error against the strongest baseline (A+G at INT4, A at INT6, chosen on the calibration half) by 21.5\% $[12.7,29.4]$ at INT4, so the 10\% criterion is met, but by 10.9\% $[-1.2,21.4]$ at INT6, where the interval includes zero and the baselines are not better than the calibration median; on all 6,464 items, a secondary analysis with the floor-normalized error (grey in Figure~\ref{fig:predict}), the INT6 reduction is 37.1\% $[30.7,43.0]$. The pre-specified utility criterion needs both states on eligible items and is not met. The same model reduces the error by 23.4\% $[15.1,30.8]$ in the rounding-only INT16 state, so the INT4 gain cannot be attributed to a quantization-specific mechanism: B also predicts pure rounding error (it measures how noise-prone a context is). On eligible items at INT4 neither gradient attribution (held-out error 0.124 against 0.125, no interval) nor the Pearson survival of the feature adds to A or to B (survival: $-0.4\%$ $[-1.8,0.7]$ and $-0.6\%$ $[-4.3,3.4]$); at INT6 adding survival to B is slightly worse ($-6.5\%$ $[-12.0,-1.6]$).

\paragraph{External panel.}
We ran the same design once on 1,800 Wikipedia passages (300 each in English, Hindi, Russian, Spanish, Yoruba and Chinese; 402 of the 404 features; same rules, floor recomputed: 1,444 of 6,406 items eligible). INT4 repeats the main result: the effect vector is rotated (cosine 0.754, normalized error 0.679), the weight term has the larger norm, the averaged fingerprint hides the error (cosine gap $+0.132$ $[0.125,0.140]$ against $+0.027$ in the rounding-only state; H4 supported), the context term of the fixed edit exceeds the weight term ($+0.053$ $[+0.040,+0.064]$, 4 of 6 languages; A3 supported), and B reduces the held-out error by 19.4\% $[5.1,31.8]$ (20.6\% $[9.3,31.2]$ when fitted on the FLORES calibration features, no refit). INT6 differs: the weight term is larger at the group level ($-0.0049$ $[-0.0060,-0.0031]$; A3 reversed), H4 is inconclusive, and the reduction is 23.4\% $[12.8,32.5]$ (against the calibration median, which beats both activation baselines at INT6, the FLORES-fitted cross-corpus reduction is 15.8\% $[-2.7,28.7]$), so the utility criterion is met here though not on FLORES; the rounding-only state is predicted as well (28.9\%). With six languages and a smaller eligible population this is an external-validity check, not a replication.

\section{Graded Feature Scaling}
Study G varies $\alpha$ in Equation~\ref{eq:edit} for the K64 groups with three down multipliers (0.3536, 0.1768, 0.0884) and their reciprocals, calibrated on a 100-sentence FP pilot, on 300 sentences, 27 languages and three control draws. In FP the median net harm rises 0.0223, 0.0902, 0.1687, 0.2624 as the group is suppressed more strongly and then zeroed (ordered in all 27 languages; 26 with a positive lower bound at the deepest down multiplier); strong amplification also raises harm; the curves are similar from FP through INT5 with an INT4 increase. The strict precision-monotonicity rule is not met and no relaxed tolerance is substituted. A follow-up on 562 reserve sentences with five control draws finds a small INT4 benefit of amplifying by 1.1 (median net harm $-0.00135$ nats/token, 97.5\% interval $[-0.00197,-0.00032]$, 20 of 27 languages; 1.2: not met), with a small harm at FP, INT6 and INT5. State-defined groups (Jaccard with the FP group 0.49 at INT4) are not more consequential ($-0.0098$), and the language-specific optimal gain is reproducible (reliability 0.917) but not explained by inverse activation shrinkage (rank correlation $-0.170$, $p=0.804$): a simple compensation explanation is not supported.

\section{A Second-Model Check}
\label{sec:llama}
We ran a minimum consistency check on Llama-3.2-1B \citep{grattafiori2024} with a public TopK \citep{gao2024} SAE (14,336 features, residual entering block 12) through an adapter around the frozen Gemma pipeline (same languages, sentences, selectivity rule, K64 groups, three control draws, Study G multipliers, 300 sentences; FP, INT8, INT6, INT5, INT4), with predictions fixed beforehand (Table~\ref{tab:llama}). \section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The original selective-destruction account is not established: primary contrasts were weak, the threshold-dependent disadvantage reversed with magnitude matching, and donor patches attributed little quantization loss to the selected groups. The main methodological lesson is to say what a survival measurement preserves: a correlation cutoff describes activation agreement, a peak label depends on a margin, and a logit fingerprint describes a sampled intervention in a changing downstream model.

The crossed design adds three findings. First, whether an intervention keeps its effect depends strongly on the precision (cosine 0.98 at INT6, 0.77 at INT4): at INT6 it changes little (about two floors, above the rounding floor) and at INT4 the effect vector is rotated rather than shrunk. Second, the answer to ``context or weights'' depends on the quantity and on the edit: for the fixed full-precision edit the group-level loss is amplified mostly through the context term, but with the quantized state's own activation the harm is close to that of the weights alone in the median language (not in every language), and the effect vector has its larger norm in the weight term; the interaction is never negligible at INT4, so a single split would mislead. Third, most of the transfer error is shared with other directions and their sensitivity predicts it (it also predicts the rounding-only error, so this is not shown to be quantization-specific); activation survival and, on eligible items at INT4, gradient attribution add nothing measurable; and averaged fingerprints hide it. Together with the earlier result that activation survival and association status are not reliable proxies for functional change, this argues against using activation survival as a proxy for the survival of interventions; it does not show that a better proxy is unavailable.

\label{main-content-end}
\section*{Limitations, Challenges, and Future Work}
All analyses use one base model, layer, frozen SAE and simulated round-to-nearest weights (not deployed integer kernels); dictionary fidelity can deteriorate after compression. Zero ablation can be off-distribution and does not establish unique necessity or semantic identity; matching is imperfect and control draws overlap. Language tiers confound script, tokenization, training data and competence. Most results are exploratory, and bootstrap intervals condition on the selected sample.

For the transfer study: the numerical floor is large (bfloat16 execution), so INT6 effects are only about two floors; ``held-out'' means held-out features (88\% of held-out source sentences also occur among calibration contexts); the group-level interval resamples sentences only and the averaged-fingerprint interval features only; the control directions of the specificity contrast are not eligibility-filtered; the ridge strength chosen was the largest of the three tried; the generic-sensitivity model also works in the rounding-only state, so its INT4 gain must not be read as quantization-specific; the gradient baseline is the scalar loss-attribution form, not the effect-vector form (post hoc, $r=0.63$ with the signed loss change, $0.58$ with its INT4 shift); and the six-language external panel is a validity check, not a replication of the 27-language study (its INT6 statuses differ and one language dominates its medians). The second-model check confounds model family, dictionary, depth and tokenizer; Gemma-2-9B with Gemma Scope was handed to another team and is not part of this report.

Challenges included replacing the initial pipeline, accelerator memory limits, hardware heterogeneity (one node with different float results, one CPU run; both caught by gates), and CUDA failures. Future work should replicate across models and quantizers (including integer kernels and activation quantization), test behavioural steering with calibrated headroom, and test the generic-sensitivity predictor against a quantization-specific alternative. These are not completed results.

\subsection*{Hypotheses That Were Not Supported}
The original selective-versus-control tests (primary regime), the frozen Belebele statement S0 (making S1--S5 undefined), strict Study G precision monotonicity, the main-study repair rules (before the larger follow-up, which supported only the 1.1 multiplier), state-defined groups being more consequential, inverse shrinkage predicting the optimal gain, on Llama-3.2-1B the predictions P1, P2b, P3a, and P3b, and in the transfer study the utility criterion on FLORES (met at INT4, not at INT6; met on the external panel) and the context-dominance expectation at INT6 (undecided on FLORES, reversed on the external panel). They are reported as outcomes and were not converted by changing thresholds.
