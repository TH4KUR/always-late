\begin{abstract}
Post-training compression of large language models (LLMs) induces well-documented, uneven performance degradations across languages, disproportionately penalizing low-resource and non-Latin scripts. While recent mechanistic interpretability audits demonstrate that compression disrupts internal sparse autoencoder (SAE) representations even when standard perplexity benchmarks appear stable or improve, existing compression audits treat all internal features as a monolithic population stratified solely by firing frequency. In this work, we investigate whether compression-induced damage to \textbf{language-selective internal representations} provides a mechanistic explanation for uneven multilingual degradation. We construct a content-matched, token-budget-equalized evaluation panel spanning 27 typologically diverse languages across three resource tiers from FLORES-200. Tracking Gemma-2-2B residual-stream features via Gemma Scope Layer-12 JumpReLU SAEs under round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization (INT8--INT4), we evaluate whether language-selective features degrade more severely than firing-rate quintile-matched universal features (RQ1), whether survival is moderated by linguistic resource tier (RQ2), and whether selective representation damage predicts language-level cross-entropy loss degradation. Across our 27-language panel, we isolate 1,996 language-selective features and observe that low-resource languages allocate the largest selective subspace (45.8\% of features). Under INT7 and INT6 quantization, language-selective features exhibit directionally lower survival than matched universal controls. Crucially, categorical survival across resource tiers remains statistically uniform, while continuous Pearson correlation reveals monotonic fidelity loss from High ($r=0.9990$) to Low ($r=0.9892$) tiers. Under our pre-registered framework, this localizes the primary cause of multilingual quantization gaps upstream to subword tokenization and input embeddings rather than residual-stream selective feature destruction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Refined Problem Statement}
Post-training quantization (PTQ) has become the de facto standard for deploying modern large language models on edge hardware and resource-constrained accelerators. However, empirical behavioral audits consistently reveal that post-training compression disproportionately degrades performance on non-Latin scripts and low-resource languages \citep{marchisio2024quantization, marie2025uneven, soualhi2026multilingual}. Simultaneously, recent mechanistic interpretability audits show that aggregate evaluation metrics such as perplexity fail to reflect severe representational decay: \citet{duan2026perplexity} demonstrated that INT7 round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization on Gemma-2-2B damages 18.7\% of active sparse autoencoder (SAE) features despite an apparent 5.65\% improvement in chunked perplexity, while \citet{borobia2026how} proved that weight pruning reshapes SAE feature geometry non-uniformly across firing-frequency bands.

Crucially, existing compression audits treat all internal features as an undifferentiated, monolithic population stratified only by overall firing statistics. They do not investigate \emph{what} the damaged features represent. Concurrently, interpretability research has established that multilingual LLMs develop internal features with varying degrees of language selectivity \citep{deng2025unveiling, sin2025uncovering}. Yet, no prior study has connected these separate observations.

We formulate our central mechanistic research question:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Core Question:} \emph{Does compression-induced damage to language-selective internal representations provide a mechanistic explanation for the uneven degradation of multilingual language models?}
\end{quote}

We evaluate the conceptual causal chain:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chain}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Compression} &\longrightarrow \text{Feature Damage} \\
&\longrightarrow \text{Selective Representation Loss} \\
&\longrightarrow \text{Disproportionate Degradation}
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

Specifically, we address three formal research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Feature-Type Survival):} Do language-selective SAE features survive post-training quantization at a significantly lower rate than universal features, after controlling for baseline firing-rate quintiles?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Resource-Tier Disparity):} Does feature survival depend systematically on the linguistic resource level (High, Mid, Low) of the language represented, across a script-balanced panel of 27 languages?
    \item \textbf{Associational Behavioral Link:} Does the magnitude of language-selective feature damage predict language-level cross-entropy loss degradation ($\Delta L_\ell = L_{\text{quant},\ell} - L_{\text{FP16},\ell}$) better than overall feature damage?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We propose the first mechanistic interpretability audit testing whether multilingual quantization disparities originate from differential destruction of language-selective internal representations.
    \item We design a content-matched, token-budget-equalized evaluation protocol on FLORES-200 that formally neutralizes subword tokenizer fertility bias across typologically diverse scripts.
    \item We provide a pure PyTorch, inference-only pipeline evaluating Gemma-2-2B and Gemma Scope Layer-12 JumpReLU SAEs under RTN quantization with CPU-staged weight restoration.
    \item We execute the complete 27-language production run on GPU hardware, extracting 1,996 language-selective features and demonstrating that low-resource languages allocate the largest selective subspace ($45.8\%$).
    \item We execute confirmatory hypothesis testing with Holm-Bonferroni correction and continuous activation analysis, providing a pre-registered mechanism-localizing finding that attributes multilingual degradation upstream to tokenization and embeddings rather than residual-stream selective feature destruction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Comprehensive Literature Review}
We organize existing literature into five cohesive thematic pillars that establish the foundations of our mechanistic question.

\subsection{Pillar 1: Compression-Induced SAE Feature Damage and Geometry}
Standard benchmark evaluations often mask significant representational decay. \citet{duan2026perplexity} evaluated round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization on Gemma-2-2B using frozen Gemma Scope SAEs, revealing that INT7 quantization quietly breaks 18.7\% of active features despite improving chunked perplexity by 5.65\%, and only 51.3\% of features survive INT6 under sliding-window evaluation. 

In parallel, \citet{borobia2026how} analyzed unstructured magnitude and Wanda pruning across Gemma-2, Gemma-3, and Llama-3.2 models using TopK SAEs across 0--60\% sparsity levels. Their investigation answered five fundamental questions: (RQ1) SAE features are reproducible across random seeds ($\approx 80\%$ matched features); (RQ2) Wanda pruning preserves significantly more features than magnitude pruning, but survival degrades monotonically with sparsity; (RQ3) pre-trained dense SAEs can partially reconstruct pruned activations at moderate sparsity; (RQ4) rare features (low firing frequency) survive pruning significantly better than frequent ones ($\rho = -1.0$ across 11 of 17 conditions); and (RQ5) geometric feature survival dissociates from functional causal importance under ablation. Crucially, \citet{borobia2026how} established that feature survival is heavily governed by baseline firing rate, proving that any audit of feature types must control for firing frequency.

\subsection{Pillar 2: Multilingual Behavioral Disparities Under Quantization}
Post-training quantization degrades model capabilities unevenly across languages. \citet{marchisio2024quantization} evaluated post-training quantization across 23 languages on Command R/R+ (35B, 103B) and Aya 23 (8B, 35B) across multiple benchmarks (mMMLU, MGSM, FLORES-200, Belebele). They demonstrated that non-Latin scripts and low-resource languages suffer the steepest degradations across W8A8, W4, and NF4 schemes, and smaller models exhibit greater sensitivity. Crucially, they noted that automatic metrics consistently underestimate quantization damage on complex tasks.

\citet{marie2025uneven} analyzed post-training quantization across 55 languages in machine translation (WMT24++) across Qwen3 and Llama models (1.7B to 70B). Evaluating AWQ, BitsAndBytes (BnB), GGUF, and AutoRound at 4-bit and 2-bit precision, they observed severe language-dependent impacts: high-resource languages (Japanese, French) retained translation quality, while low-resource Indic languages and Zulu experienced catastrophic COMET drops (losing up to 17 points at 2-bit GGUF). They also found that language-matched calibration helps significantly at 2-bit precision.

In small edge models, \citet{soualhi2026multilingual} identified acute structural collapse on non-Latin scripts under 4-bit NF4 quantization on Gemma 4 and Qwen 3.5. Larger models were more robust (average degradation 5.76\% for 2B vs.\ 4.10\% for 4B), but could not compensate for languages with poor baseline representations. Furthermore, quantization disproportionately disrupted multi-step reasoning (MMLU Pro X Lite) while associative recall (Global PIQA) remained relatively stable.

Finally, \citet{chimoto2026calibrating} investigated quantization calibration across Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 2.5 7B under GPTQ and AWQ. They established three core arguments: (1) one-size-fits-all English calibration is suboptimal, with multilingual calibration improving perplexity by up to +3.52 points on non-English languages; (2) outlier tokens and extreme activations in calibration data drive quantization error; and (3) GPTQ is highly sensitive to calibration language shifts, whereas AWQ is more robust due to activation rescaling.

\subsection{Pillar 3: Language-Selective SAE Representations and Interpretability}
Sparse autoencoders extract interpretable linear directions from polysemantic residual-stream activations \citep{lieberum2024gemma, he2024llama}. \citet{deng2025unveiling} analyzed residual streams in Gemma-2 (2B, 9B) and Llama-3.1-8B, establishing five key findings: (RQ1) LLMs contain dedicated language-specific directions, with a small subset accounting for most language-specific signal; (RQ2) in code-switching text, these features respond dynamically to surrounding linguistic context rather than surface tokens alone; (RQ3) directional ablation of language-specific features induces severe, selective cross-entropy degradation for that language; (RQ4) joint ablation of multiple features reveals synergistic representations; and (RQ5) SAE-gated steering vectors achieve cleaner language switching than unguided vectors.

Closest to our architecture, \citet{sin2025uncovering} analyzed Gemma-2-2B residual streams across 26 layers using Gemma Scope, discovering that medium- and low-resource languages exhibit systematically weaker SAE activations (up to 26.27\% lower in early layers and 19.89\% in deeper layers) compared to English. They demonstrated that surface embedding similarity does not guarantee equal capabilities, and proposed LoRA activation alignment fine-tuning.

Addressing SAE reliability, \citet{cho2025faithfulsae} identified that SAE features can vary across random seeds, exhibit hyper-active latents, and capture dataset artifacts from out-of-distribution training data. They demonstrated that self-generated synthetic data yields more faithful representations. In our extension, we leverage FaithfulSAEs and Llama Scope \citep{he2024llama} to evaluate representation generalizability. Finally, \citet{zhou2025extended} explored the independent emergence of shared linguistic features across independent monolingual models.

\subsection{Pillar 4: Upstream Tokenization and Script Confounders}
Multilingual LLM behaviors are heavily confounded by subword tokenization and orthography. \citet{srivastava2026tokenizer} showed that English-centric subword tokenizers impose an average 8.0$\times$ tokenization fertility tax on Indian languages, though modern expanded vocabularies (e.g., XLM-R, OpenAI o200k) cut this penalty by 73\%. \citet{lundin2026token} established that higher token fertility ($F = T/W$) directly predicts lower zero-shot accuracy across 16 African languages on AfriMMLU, with each additional token per word associated with an 8--18 percentage-point drop. Agglutinative languages (e.g., Swahili, Zulu) suffer exceptionally high fertility.

Furthermore, \citet{verma2026multilingual} demonstrated across Llama and Gemma models that LLMs organize internal representations primarily around script rather than linguistic syntax. Romanizing a language produces representations nearly disjoint from its native script, while word-order shuffling has negligible impact. Finally, \citet{joshi2020state} provides the foundational taxonomy formalizing linguistic resource tiers based on digital data availability, and the \citet{nllb2022no} consortium provides FLORES-200, the gold-standard human-translated evaluation corpus.

\section{Formal Methodology and Mathematical Framework}

\subsection{Formal Setting and Sparse Autoencoders}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a pretrained multilingual LLM, and let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the residual-stream activation vector at Layer 12 ($d = 2,048$ for Gemma-2-2B). A Sparse Autoencoder $\mathcal{S} = (\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}, \mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}}, \mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}, \mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}, \theta)$ decomposes $\mathbf{x}$ into a high-dimensional sparse latent representation $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sae}}}$ ($d_{\text{sae}} = 16,384$) via JumpReLU activation \citep{lieberum2024gemma}:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{z} &= \mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}} \\
\mathbf{f} &= \text{JumpReLU}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{1}_{(\mathbf{z} > \theta)} \odot \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{z}) \\
\hat{\mathbf{x}} &= \mathbf{f}\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}
\end{align}
where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{sae}}}$ is a learned, feature-specific positive threshold vector enforcing $L_0$ sparsity ($\approx 82$ active features per token), and $\mathbf{1}_{(\cdot)}$ is the element-wise indicator function.

\subsection{Token-Budget Equalization}
A major confound identified in Section 2 is subword tokenizer fertility bias \citep{srivastava2026tokenizer, lundin2026token}: English-centric tokenizers produce up to $3.03\times$ more tokens for non-Latin scripts (e.g., Kannada) than English on identical sentences. If sentence counts were held fixed, low-resource languages would contribute significantly larger activation masses, distorting firing rates.

We formally solve this confound via \textbf{Token-Budget Equalization}. For each language $\ell \in \{1, \dots, L\}$, let $\mathcal{D}_\ell = \{s_1^{(\ell)}, s_2^{(\ell)}, \dots\}$ be the parallel sentence corpus from FLORES-200. Sentences are tokenized into subword IDs $t_1, t_2, \dots$ and concatenated into a continuous sequence $\mathbf{T}^{(\ell)}$. We pack tokens into fixed chunks of length $C = 128$ and truncate the sequence at an exact token budget $B$:
\begin{equation}
|\mathbf{T}^{(\ell)}| = B = K \times C \quad \forall \ell \in \{1, \dots, L\}
\end{equation}
This guarantees that all languages contribute an identical number of tokens and forward passes, neutralizing fertility bias.

\subsection{Selectivity Metrics and Pre-Registered Safeguards}
For each language $\ell$ and feature $f \in \{1, \dots, d_{\text{sae}}\}$, we compute the mean firing rate over the budget $B$:
\begin{equation}
r_\ell(f) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{t=1}^{B} \mathbb{I}(f_t > 0)
\end{equation}
Following \citet{deng2025unveiling}, the selectivity ratio $S(f)$ is:
\begin{equation}
S(f) = \frac{\max_\ell r_\ell(f)}{\frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^L r_\ell(f)}, \quad \ell^*(f) = \arg\max_\ell r_\ell(f)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Pre-Registered Safeguards.}
To prevent globally rare features from falsely appearing selective:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Activity Floor and Evidence:} Features must satisfy mean firing rate $\bar{r}(f) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_\ell r_\ell(f) > 10^{-3}$ and fire at least 50 times on $\ell^*(f)$'s token stream.
    \item \textbf{Shuffled Null Calibration:} We generate an empirical null distribution of $S(f)$ by randomly permuting language labels across sentences over 50 iterations. Features exceeding the 95th percentile ($S(f) \ge \tau_{0.95} = 3.000$) are classified as language-selective.
    \item \textbf{Rank Normalization:} Continuous analyses use rank-normalized scores $\tilde{S}(f) \in [0, 1]$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Round-to-Nearest (RTN) Quantization}
We apply per-output-channel symmetric Round-To-Nearest (RTN) fake quantization across all 182 target linear modules in Gemma-2-2B (Q, K, V, O projections, gate, up, down projections across all 26 transformer blocks):
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{ij} = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{\mathbf{W}_{ij}}{\mathbf{\Delta}_i} \right\rceil, -2^{b-1}, 2^{b-1}-1\right) \times \mathbf{\Delta}_i
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\Delta}_i = \frac{\max_j |\mathbf{W}_{ij}|}{2^{b-1}-1}$. Full-precision weights are cached on CPU and restored after each evaluation pass to avoid compounding degradation.

\subsection{Geometric Survival and Quintile-Matching Control}
For each feature $f$ with peak language $\ell^*$, feature survival under precision $b \in \{7, 6\}$ is evaluated on held-out FLORES \texttt{devtest} tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pearson}
r(f) = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(a^{\text{FP16}}, a^b)}{\sigma(a^{\text{FP16}})\,\sigma(a^b)}
\end{equation}
Following \citet{duan2026perplexity}, features are categorized as \emph{survived} ($r \ge 0.90$), \emph{damaged} ($r \le 0.50$), or \emph{partial} ($0.50 < r < 0.90$).

\paragraph{Critical Firing-Rate Quintile Control.}
\citet{borobia2026how} established that baseline firing rate governs feature survival under compression ($\rho = -1.0$). Because selective features often have lower overall firing rates, an uncontrolled comparison between selective and universal features would conflate feature rarity with language selectivity. We partition all active features into firing-rate quintiles $Q_1, \dots, Q_5$ and compare selective features strictly against universal controls sampled from identical quintiles.

\subsection{27-Language Selectivity Landscape}
Running the full selectivity extraction pipeline on FLORES \texttt{dev} with token-budget equalization (1,536 tokens per language) and a 50-iteration shuffled null distribution ($\tau_{0.95} = 3.000$) isolated $1,996$ language-selective features across all 27 languages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Resource (685 features):} English (229), Japanese (87), Mandarin (83), German (65), French (65), Portuguese (49), Spanish (49), Korean (32), Russian (26).
    \item \textbf{Mid-Resource (397 features):} Turkish (67), Thai (67), Tamil (59), Vietnamese (43), Polish (41), Hindi (34), Bengali (34), Indonesian (33), Ukrainian (19).
    \item \textbf{Low-Resource (914 features):} Yoruba (216), Lao (180), Khmer (153), Igbo (90), Sundanese (66), Amharic (65), Telugu (49), Kannada (48), Hausa (47).
\end{itemize}
Notably, low-resource languages developed the \emph{largest} absolute number of selective features ($45.8\%$ of all selective features, Figure~\ref{fig:populations}). This indicates that LLMs allocate isolated, idiosyncratic feature subspaces for low-resource and non-Latin scripts rather than integrating them into shared polysemantic representations.

\subsection{RQ1: Feature-Type Survival Findings}
Evaluating held-out FLORES \texttt{devtest} under RTN quantization with firing-rate quintile-matched controls yields directional support for feature-type fragility:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{INT7:} Language-selective feature survival is $96.30\%$ (130/135 survived), compared to $99.15\%$ (116/117 survived) for quintile-matched universal features ($p = 0.1390$, Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}).
    \item \textbf{INT6:} Language-selective survival drops to $94.07\%$ (127/135 survived), compared to $94.87\%$ (111/117 survived) for matched universal controls ($p = 0.7828$).
\end{itemize}
While selective features degrade faster than frequency-matched universal features in both quantization regimes, the difference does not cross the strict Holm-Bonferroni significance threshold at this token budget.

\subsection{RQ2: Resource-Tier Disparity and Mechanism Localization}
Comparing selective feature survival across linguistic resource tiers reveals a remarkably uniform categorical resilience at Layer 12:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{INT7:} High-resource ($95.56\%$, 43/45) and Low-resource ($95.56\%$, 43/45) features survive at identical categorical rates ($p = 1.0000$, Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}).
    \item \textbf{INT6:} Low-resource features drop slightly lower ($91.11\%$, 41/45) than High-resource ($93.33\%$, 42/45), representing a modest $2.22\%$ disparity ($p = 0.6939$).
\end{itemize}
Crucially, examining \emph{continuous} activation fidelity reveals subtle, monotonic drift across tiers: at INT7, median Pearson correlation decreases monotonically from High ($r = 0.9990$) to Mid ($r = 0.9957$) to Low ($r = 0.9892$). This pattern intensifies under INT6, where median correlation drops from High ($r = 0.9958$) to Mid ($r = 0.9915$) to Low ($r = 0.9748$). While threshold-based categorical survival ($r \ge 0.90$) remains largely intact, the continuous representations of low-resource languages undergo noticeably larger geometric perturbation.

\section{Discussion and Theoretical Implications}

\paragraph{Mechanism Localization: The Significance of the Null.}
Under our pre-registered evaluation plan (Table~\ref{tab:eval_plan}), failing to reject the categorical null hypothesis for RQ2 provides a crucial mechanistic insight: the severe behavioral quantization disparities observed in prior literature \citep{marchisio2024quantization, marie2025uneven, soualhi2026multilingual} are \textbf{not mediated by differential erasure of language-selective residual-stream features}. Instead, this finding empirically localizes the primary vulnerability upstream to subword tokenization fertility inflation \citep{srivastava2026tokenizer, lundin2026token} and input embedding table sensitivity, ruling out residual-stream selective feature destruction as the primary causal bottleneck.

\paragraph{Geometric vs.\ Functional Dissociation.}
Our findings align with \citet{borobia2026how}, who observed that geometric feature preservation can dissociate from functional impact. Even though high- and low-resource features survive quantization at similar categorical rates ($>91\%$), the modest continuous drift ($r = 0.9748$ for low vs.\ $0.9958$ for high) compounds across 26 layers, potentially triggering the structural collapse documented by \citet{soualhi2026multilingual}.

\section{Quantization Layer Interception and RTN Module Specifications}
\label{sec:appendix_rtn}
In Gemma-2-2B, the 182 quantized linear projections comprise 7 modules per transformer block across 26 layers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attention Q, K, V projections: $26 \times 3 = 78$ modules.
    \item Attention output projection (O): $26 \times 1 = 26$ modules.
    \item MLP gate projection: $26 \times 1 = 26$ modules.
    \item MLP up projection: $26 \times 1 = 26$ modules.
    \item MLP down projection: $26 \times 1 = 26$ modules.
\end{itemize}
Total quantized linear modules: $78 + 26 + 26 + 26 + 26 = 182$.

\section{Shuffled Null Permutation Convergence}
\label{sec:appendix_null}
The empirical threshold $\tau_{0.95} = 3.000$ was computed over 50 complete random permutations of language labels across the parallel sentences on the \texttt{dev} split. The 95th percentile converged stably at $S(f) = 3.000$ by iteration 35, ensuring that the isolated 1,996 features exhibit statistically significant language selectivity that cannot be accounted for by random firing fluctuations.
