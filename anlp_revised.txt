Motivation & Impact

Deployment practitioners choosing a quantization or pruning scheme for a multilingual model
currently have no signal that a given compression
level might be quietly degrading service quality
for specific languages while perplexity and English
benchmarks look untouched, this is precisely the
blind spot Duan (2026) demonstrate in the monolingual case, and we test whether it has a linguisticequity dimension. Interpretability researchers
obtain the first test of whether the pruning finding, that rare features survive better than frequent
ones (Borobia et al., 2026), holds, is reversed, or is
orthogonal to language-selectivity, since languagespecific features are plausibly rare-firing by construction (they fire only on a subset of a multilingual corpus) and this creates a directly testable
tension between two candidate predictors of survival. Low-resource-language NLP inherits a concrete audit tool: if the effect is present, “safe to
quantize” claims about multilingual models need a
per-language qualifier, not only an aggregate one.
Given the behavioral evidence above, a null result
is equally reportable and arguably more consequential: it would indicate that the documented multilingual quantization tax does not arise through
erasure of language-specific features, redirecting
attention to pre-compression causes such as tokenizer fertility and stranded-byte costs (Srivastava,
2026; Lundin et al., 2026).

Background & What We Add

SAE feature survival under compression.
Borobia et al. (2026) is the first systematic study
of how unstructured pruning reshapes SAE feature
geometry, across three model families, two pruning methods (magnitude, Wanda), and six sparsity
levels; their central finding is that firing-rate quintile predicts survival, with rare features preferentially preserved, and that geometric survival does
not necessarily predict causal importance under
ablation. Duan (2026) apply the same audit lens
to round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization on Pythia70M and Gemma-2-2B, using a frozen SAE as a

fixed measurement basis and Pearson correlation
of per-feature activation across bit-widths (INT8–
INT4) as the survival metric; under their chunked
protocol INT7 improves perplexity (∆ = −5.65%)
and INT6 worsens it modestly (+3.99%), while
under a stronger sliding-window protocol both improve (−6.49%, −3.45%); they additionally show
that quantization and matched-perplexity pruning
damage strongly overlapping feature sets (Jaccard
0.79–0.86). Both studies treat all active features
as members of one population, stratified only by
firing statistics, neither stratifies by what a feature
represents, and neither includes multilingual text
in its evaluation corpus.
Language-specific, universal, and disparatelyactivated SAE features. Deng et al. (2025) identify language-specific directions in LLM activation
space via sparse autoencoders and show they can be
used for targeted language steering, distinct from
shared semantic features. Zhou and Salhan (2025)
outline a design for testing whether independently
trained monolingual (Goldfish) models converge
on shared feature structure; published convergence
results were not yet available at writing. Closest
to our setup, Sin Jing Xuan et al. (2025) decompose Gemma-2-2B activations with Gemma Scope
SAEs across all 26 residual layers and ten languages, reporting systematic activation-magnitude
disparities: medium-to-low-resource languages receive up to 26.27% lower activations in early layers,
persisting at 19.89% even deep. None of these papers studies compression; Deng et al. provide the
selectivity methodology this proposal repurposes
as a stratification variable, and Sin Jing Xuan et
al. establish on our primary model that languageassociated SAE structure is already uneven, sharpening our question to whether compression preferentially damages that uneven structure.
Multilingual fragility more broadly. Independent of compression, multilingual LLMs already
treat languages unevenly at the tokenization and
representational level: fertility and stranded-byte
rates vary by orders of magnitude across scripts
(Srivastava, 2026), no single intrinsic tokenizer
metric predicts English performance, while singletoken retention rate significantly predicts multilingual accuracy (Chiu, 2026), per-token accuracy costs of 8–18 points have been measured
for African languages (Lundin et al., 2026), tokenizer choice is itself a contested proxy for downstream quality (Ali et al., 2024), and representa-

4.4

Phase 2: Causal Check (RQ3, Extension
Only)

For the top-k most language-selective features per
language (by S̃(f )), we run targeted ablation on
both full-precision and compressed models, measuring the change in per-language next-token loss
on held-out FLORES-200 continuations. This
tests whether geometric survival (Phase 1) predicts
functional importance post-compression, directly
extending Borobia et al.’s finding that these can
dissociate, now asking whether the dissociation
differs for low-resource-language features. Consistent with our framing, a Phase-2 null would
support the interpretation that the behavioral tax
arises upstream of selective feature loss (e.g., tokenizer fertility costs, scattered across many weaklydamaged features) rather than through erasure of a
few language-critical features. This phase is staged
as an extension: it strengthens the paper but is not
required for a complete, submittable result.

Models and SAEs. Primary: Gemma-2-2B with

as specified in §4.3. All SAEs are pretrained and

(Gemma, Llama); no ethical-review- relevant data

Data. FLORES-200 dev/devtest parallel sentences (NLLB Team, 2022) provide contentmatched text; because FLORES ships language
codes rather than resource-tier labels, tiers are
assigned by an external taxonomy, Joshi et
al.’s (2020) classes, cross-checked against NLLB
training-corpus mass, and the assignment table is
frozen in the OSF registration. Candidate panel of
27 languages, nine per tier, balanced across scripts:
high-resource eng, deu, spa, fra, por, rus, cmnHans, jpn, kor; mid-resource hin, ben, tam, tur,
vie, ind, tha, pol, ukr; low-resource kan, tel, yor,
sun, amh, ibo, hau, khm, lao (FLORES-200 language codes abbreviated). Any pre-tagging replacement must preserve tier membership and maintain
at least four distinct scripts per tier, recorded before registration. No native-speaker annotation or
gold-concept curation is required.
Hardware. All operations are forward passes
with SAE encode/decode hooks; nothing requires
gradients. This fits comfortably within four 11 GB
GPUs in bf16. Estimated total: 30–50 GPU-hours
for the guaranteed core (equal-budget encoding of
the 27-language dev/devtest panel × six precision
states; compressed checkpoints are reused across
languages), and a further 20–30 GPU-hours for
extensions, undertaken only when the core finishes
with buffer remaining.

Release. Selectivity scores, per-feature survival
tables, and analysis code are released in full; no
proprietary or non-public data is used at any stage.
